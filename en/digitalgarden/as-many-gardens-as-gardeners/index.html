As Many Gardens as Gardeners

When I started looking into the idea of the digital garden, I realised the magic lies in the variety of perspectives. Everyone who has written or spoken about it adds their own piece, and all those voices coexist without contradicting one another.

After reading and listening to the people who have thought about the digital garden, I see three eras.

The thinkers of the garden's structure

The idea begins in the world of web design. In 1998, Mark Bernstein published Hypertext Gardens, where he proposed thinking of the web not as a highway (efficient, fast, directed) but as a garden: a place that invites you to stay, explore, reflect.

Twenty years later, Maggie Appleton picked the idea up and developed it in a wonderful essay, A Brief History & Ethos of the Digital Garden. She identifies six patterns that distinguish a garden from a blog: topography over timelines, continuous growth, imperfection and learning in public, playful experimentation, content diversity, and independent ownership. Maggie has also written about the cozy web: the private corners of the internet (communities on Discord, Twitter, Telegram, Patreon and elsewhere) that we have retreated into in recent years. The digital garden is, in part, a way out of the cozy web that doesn't drop you back into the noise of the feed.

In 2018, Tom Critchlow added another metaphor: campfires. If feeds are streams and gardens are cultivation, campfires are the intimate conversations between people who read each other. I love his way of describing the garden as "a blog without the publish button."

Joel Hooks insisted on process. Ideas grow slowly, in the open, while you write and rewrite. You don't wait for the final answer to publish it.

The voices of attention

Mike Caulfield was probably the first to articulate the modern concept. In a 2015 talk, he laid out the opposition that is still central: the garden and the stream. The stream is Twitter, emails, chats — information that passes through. The garden works like Wikipedia: information that connects, deepens, is maintained.

Anne-Laure Le Cunff connects it with the cultivation of curiosity. For her, the digital garden is a mental space where you can keep thinking about something for months without productivity pressure. It's coherent with her critique of the trap of measuring learning as if it were work.

Several more recent voices on YouTube are extending this. One of them is Anna Howard. On her podcast Wild Geese, she talks about the garden as an antidote to doomscrolling: a way of moving from passive consumer to active creator, of not letting ideas evaporate.

The political critique

A third view — Alba Lafarga's — reads the digital garden as a gesture of resistance against the degradation of the internet and the cultural flattening produced by algorithms. Against virality, slowness. She picks up Bernstein and Caulfield to anchor the idea historically, and connects it with Byung-Chul Han's ethics of inactivity, the fediverse (decentralised social networks like Mastodon or Pixelfed) and the right to inhabit the internet differently. She presents the digital garden as a non-performative space, without metrics, against the culture of performance.

There are as many ways of keeping a digital garden as there are people willing to plant and tend one.
