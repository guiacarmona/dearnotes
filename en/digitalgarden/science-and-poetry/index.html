On science and poetry

Maria Popova frames science and poetry as two modes of meeting reality. Science meets reality on its own terms — the work of truth. Poetry broadens the terms on which we meet ourselves and each other — the work of meaning. Between the two, she says, sits the sum of human experience.

Knowing the physics of starlight deepens the wonder of the rainbow. Understanding aerodynamics makes the flight of a bird more astonishing.

The two modes have also fed each other historically. The language of science used to be the language of poetry. 19th century ornithologists wrote of "bewildering" birds; naturalists illustrated their own work; poets attended scientific lectures, and scientists kept poetry in their journals. Galileo's training in perspective drawing let him see the moon as material: rugged, dimensional, made of the same matter as the earth. A way of seeing developed in art transformed what science could know.

What we now call specialization is also a fragmentation of seeing. We are trained to see only what our discipline sees, and to mistake that for the full picture.

Poetry opens what Popova calls back doors of consciousness, giving access to experience the analytical mind cannot name. Sometimes it opens portals of sight we did not know we had.

Both truth and meaning reach us through language, and language has limits. They are the limits of how we describe the world. The world itself exceeds them. The standing danger of any practice built on words is mistaking the description for the reality.

Source: Maria Popova interviewed by David Perell, How to Write Something Truly Wise, May 2026.
